The Digital Millennium Copyright Act created a notice-and-takedown framework used by many online service providers in the United States. A rights holder or authorized agent can notify a provider about material alleged to infringe copyright, and the provider evaluates the request under its policy and applicable law.

## Before a notice is submitted

The sender should verify ownership or authority, identify the original work, preserve the reported page, and consider whether the use may be licensed or legally permitted. The correct recipient may be a platform portal, marketplace rights system, hosting provider, or designated DMCA agent.

## Information in a notice

A notice generally identifies the copyrighted work, the allegedly infringing material and its exact location, the complaining party's contact information, required good-faith and accuracy statements, and a signature. Platform forms may request additional fields.

Precision matters. A direct content URL is more useful than a homepage, and each allegation should connect a protected work to a specific reported location.

## What happens next

The provider may remove or restrict access, reject the notice, or request clarification. The uploader may have a counter-notice process. A counter-notice can create deadlines and legal implications, so rights holders should have an escalation plan before starting enforcement.

Keep the submitted notice, case number, response, and outcome. This record supports follow-up, relisting review, and consistent decision-making.
